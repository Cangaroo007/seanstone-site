# Part 1 — Prompts to run in your other environments

Copy each into a Cowork session that has access to the system named. Each one asks for a
**written artefact**, not a chat, so paste the output straight back to me.

### Prompt A — Road Runner: the product truth

> You have access to the Road Runner platform and codebase. I need a factual product brief for a
> public-facing knowledge base — it will be used to answer prospects' questions, so accuracy
> matters more than polish and anything uncertain must be marked as uncertain.
>
> Produce a markdown document covering:
> 1. What Road Runner actually does today, feature by feature, separating **shipped and in use**
>    from **built but unused** from **planned**.
> 2. The architecture in plain English: what it connects to, in which direction, what it reads
>    versus writes, and where it sits relative to the CRM.
> 3. Every real number you can verify from the system itself — accounts, landing pages,
>    organisations, records, enquiries processed, integrations live. Give the date each number
>    was true. Do not estimate; if you cannot verify it, list it as unverified.
> 4. The three things it does that a stock HubSpot or Webflow setup genuinely cannot.
> 5. Its current limitations, honestly. What breaks, what is manual, what would not survive a
>    much larger client.
> 6. Any client-identifying data that appears anywhere in the above, flagged separately so it can
>    be removed.
>
> Write it as a reference document, not marketing copy.

### Prompt B — Kervio: the product truth

> You have access to Kervio. I need a factual product brief for a public-facing knowledge base
> used to answer prospects' questions. Accuracy over polish; mark anything uncertain.
>
> Produce a markdown document covering:
> 1. The quote-to-manufacture workflow as the software actually implements it, step by step —
>    what a fabricator does, in order, and what the system does at each step.
> 2. What is live in production versus built versus planned.
> 3. The pricing model (per-seat / per-quote / per-shop) and the reasoning behind that structure.
> 4. Verifiable numbers: tenants, quotes processed, jobs, users, uptime — with the date each was
>    true. No estimates.
> 5. The geometry and vision components at a level safe to describe publicly, given the
>    provisional-patent workstream. Flag anything that should not be described at all.
> 6. What was genuinely hard to build and why — the parts where the AI-directed method needed the
>    most human correction. This is the most useful section for me; be specific.
> 7. Any tenant or customer names appearing anywhere above, flagged separately.

### Prompt C — the engagement inventory

*Run this wherever your client work lives — HubSpot, Drive, your notes, whatever holds the history.*

> I need an inventory of past client engagements for a knowledge base that will answer prospects'
> questions about my track record.
>
> For each engagement you can find, give me:
> - **Anonymised descriptor** — "a 40-person B2B SaaS company selling into mid-market retail",
>   never the name.
> - **The presenting problem** in one sentence, as the client described it.
> - **What it actually turned out to be** — the diagnosis, if it differed from the complaint.
> - **What was built or changed.**
> - **What measurably changed afterwards**, with the number and the timeframe. If no number
>   exists, say so rather than characterising it.
> - **How long it took**, in hours or weeks.
> - **What I would do differently**, if there is any record of that.
>
> Sort by how well-evidenced each one is. Put the ones with real numbers at the top. Flag any
> where the client name is unavoidable in the telling.

### Prompt D — how I write

*Run this in a session with your email and documents.*

> Find 8–12 examples of my own writing where I am explaining how I think about revenue systems,
> go-to-market, or a client's problem — proposal sections, emails to prospects, LinkedIn posts,
> internal memos, anything where I am making an argument rather than arranging a meeting.
>
> Return each one verbatim, with a one-line note on what it was and who it was for. Do not
> summarise or improve them — I need the actual sentences, including the ones I would edit now.
>
> Then, separately, a short analysis: recurring phrases, the structure of my arguments, how I
> open, how I disagree with someone, what I do instead of hedging.

### Prompt E — proposals and scopes

> Find every proposal, statement of work, scope document or pricing sheet I have written.
>
> For each: the client type (anonymised), the problem it addressed, the structure of the scope,
> how the work was broken into phases, the hours or fees, and the terms.
>
> Then tell me what is consistent across all of them — how I habitually structure an engagement —
> and where I have been inconsistent. The inconsistencies are as useful as the patterns.

# Part 2 — Questions only you can answer

Answer in any order. Two or three sentences each is plenty; where you have a story, tell the
story, because the stories are the thing the knowledge base most lacks. Skip anything that does
not land.

## The work itself

1. Describe the worst revenue system you have ever walked into. What was actually wrong with it?
2. What is the biggest number you have personally moved, and over what period? Even
   unattributable, even rough.
3. What is the most common thing you find that the client had no idea was happening?
4. Tell me about an engagement that did not work. What went wrong, and whose fault was it?
5. Which client said no to you, and were they right to?
6. What is the fastest you have ever produced something valuable for a client, and what was it?
7. What do you do in the first hour of looking at someone's CRM?
8. What does a genuinely good CRM look like, in a sentence a non-technical founder would follow?

## Judgement and opinion

9. What does nearly everyone get wrong about revenue operations?
10. What piece of conventional GTM advice do you think is actively harmful?
11. When is the honest answer to a prospect "you don't need me"?
12. What is the question a prospect asks that tells you immediately they are not a fit?
13. What is the question you wish more prospects asked?
14. Where has AI-directed building genuinely disappointed you?
15. What will be different about this work in three years?

## Commercial

16. Why $200 an hour, in your own words? What is the argument you would make to someone who
    flinched at it?
17. What do you refuse to charge for?
18. Have you ever refused work you could have taken? Why?
19. What does a client have to do for the engagement to go well? What is their half of it?
20. When does someone need a full-time hire instead of you?

## Road Runner and Kervio

21. What is the moment you knew Road Runner was worth building?
22. What does Road Runner do that you have never successfully explained to anyone? Say it badly
    here and I will work on it.
23. Why stone fabrication, of all industries?
24. What did building Kervio teach you that you now apply to client work?
25. What would you build next, if someone paid for it tomorrow?

## The personal register

26. What do people consistently misunderstand about you professionally?
27. What are you actually bad at that you would tell a prospect about unprompted?
28. Why does this work interest you after twenty-five years?

## What I do with the answers

Each prompt output becomes a factual section — verified numbers, real architecture, real
engagement patterns. Each answer becomes either a position in the "what he believes" section, a
worked example the engine can reach for, or a correction to something I inferred wrongly.

The specific gaps I am trying to close, in priority order:

1. **Named or anonymised client outcomes with numbers.** The single biggest hole. Question 2 and
   Prompt C.
2. **Stories.** The engine can explain your method and cannot tell one story about doing it.
   Questions 1, 3, 4, 6, 21.
3. **Your register in your own words**, rather than reconstructed from documents about you.
   Prompt D and questions 26–28.
4. **Product ground truth** so the engine never overstates what Road Runner or Kervio does.
   Prompts A and B.

Send back whatever you have. Partial is fine and immediately useful — one good story is worth
more than a complete set of short answers.
